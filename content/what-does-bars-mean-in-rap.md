In rap, a bar is one measure of music: four beats in the 4/4 grid nearly every rap beat is built on. "Bars" also means the lyrics themselves, especially good ones, as in "he's got bars." A hot 16 is a 16-bar verse, the standard length for a guest feature.

That double meaning is the whole reason this term confuses people. One sense is a unit of time you can count on your fingers. The other is a compliment. They coexist in the same sentence constantly and nobody stops to explain which one they mean.

## A bar is a measure of music. That is the literal meaning.

Written music gets chopped into measures by vertical lines on the staff. Merriam-Webster defines that line as "a vertical line across the musical staff before the initial measure accent," and lists "measure" as a separate sense of the same word. Musicians in the UK say bar. Musicians in the US often say measure. Same object.

The time signature tells you how many beats live inside each one. Rap runs on 4/4 almost exclusively, which means four beats per bar. Count "one, two, three, four" over any rap song you like and you have just counted a bar.

So when a producer says the beat drops at bar 9, that is a coordinate. It is not a value judgment. It is the same kind of statement as "turn to page 9."

## "He's got bars" is the slang meaning, and it's newer

Somewhere along the way the container became the contents. If a verse is measured in bars, then the words inside those bars are your bars, and a rapper with good ones has bars.

Dictionary.com's slang entry pins the term as hip-hop shorthand for "a rapper's lyrics, especially when considered extremely good," and notes it spread widely through the 1990s and 2000s before Urban Dictionary logged it in 2003. That timeline tracks. The word shows up in freestyle and battle circles first, where an audience shouting "bars" is doing the same job as a jazz crowd applauding mid-solo.

One practical note: in the slang sense the word is almost always plural and almost always a compliment. Nobody says "his bar was decent." "Spitting bars" and "dropping bars" both just mean rapping, with the implication that it is worth hearing.

## What is a hot 16?

A hot 16 is a 16-bar verse, delivered well. When a rapper is asked to guest on somebody else's record, the ask is usually literally phrased that way: send me a hot 16.

Wikipedia's article on rap flow states it plainly, noting that "16-bars" refers to the standard duration given for guest verses, with one bar equal to four beats of music. That is the industry's default unit of trade. It is what gets negotiated, invoiced and delivered.

The word "hot" is doing real work in the phrase. Sixteen bars is the size of the box. Hot is the standard for what you put in it.

## How long is 16 bars, actually? Here is the math.

Sixteen bars at four beats each is 64 beats. Tempo is measured in beats per minute, so the arithmetic is short:

**seconds = (bars × 4 × 60) ÷ BPM**

Which simplifies, for any 4/4 song, to **240 × bars ÷ BPM**. For a 16, that is 3,840 ÷ BPM.

| Tempo | Length of 16 bars |
|---|---|
| 70 BPM | about 54.9 seconds |
| 80 BPM | exactly 48 seconds |
| 90 BPM | about 42.7 seconds |
| 100 BPM | about 38.4 seconds |
| 120 BPM | exactly 32 seconds |
| 140 BPM | about 27.4 seconds |

So a hot 16 on a mid-tempo beat is roughly 40 to 50 seconds of rapping. Three of those, plus three 8-bar hooks and a short intro and outro, gives you 80 bars, which at 90 BPM lands at 3 minutes 33 seconds. That is not a coincidence. That is why rap songs are the length they are.

### The trap asterisk

Trap complicates the count in a way that trips up people reading a session file for the first time. Wikipedia's trap music entry notes typical tempos around 70 BPM heard, 140 BPM programmed, because producers write at the doubled tempo "to achieve finer hi-hat subdivisions."

At 140 BPM the snare lands every other bar rather than on 2 and 4, so what your body feels as one bar is two bars in the software. A rapper counting a 16 by feel on that beat is counting 32 notated bars, which brings you right back to about 54.9 seconds. The number on the screen and the number in the room disagree. Both are correct.

## How bars sit on the beat

Put on a boom-bap record and count four. The kick usually lands on 1 and 3, the snare on 2 and 4. That is the grid.

Bars then stack into phrases. Four bars is the smallest unit that feels like a complete musical thought, eight bars feels like a section, and 16 feels like a statement. This is not a rap invention. The twelve-bar blues is three groups of four. The 32-bar AABA form that ran American popular song from roughly 1925 through the early rock era is four eight-bar sections. Hip-hop inherited the powers of two and never gave them back.

A rapper's relationship to that grid is most of what we mean by flow. Wikipedia's flow entry credits Rakim with the shift from simple early patterns to modern complexity, quoting Kool Moe Dee: "Rakim created flow! We were not even using the word flow until Rakim came along." Rhymes landing before the beat, after the beat, across the barline. Same 16 bars, wildly different results.

Ad-libs live on that grid too, filling the gaps a rapper leaves at the end of a phrase. That's a whole discipline of its own, covered in our [breakdown of what rap ad-libs actually do](/rap-ad-libs-explained).

## What does MC mean in rap?

MC stands for master of ceremonies. BlackPast's entry on the term describes the original 1970s Bronx role bluntly: the DJ was the attraction, and "the MC was simply on hand to get the crowd excited about the DJ and to announce upcoming appearances." You needed the DJ's permission to touch the microphone.

Coke La Rock, an original member of DJ Kool Herc's crew the Herculoids, is often credited as one of the first MCs, present at Herc's 1973 party that gets treated as hip-hop's founding event. His raps were improvised shout-outs to friends before they were anything more literary.

The balance flipped fast. "Rapper's Delight," released September 16, 1979 on Sugar Hill Records over Chic's "Good Times," reached No. 36 on the Hot 100 and put the voice, not the turntable, at the front of the record. The abbreviation picked up backronyms afterward: microphone controller, move the crowd, mic checka. All later reverse-engineering of a term that started as a job title.

Plenty of rappers built the title straight into their names, which is its own rabbit hole worth following in our guide to [the given names behind rap's stage names](/rappers-real-names).

## What does 808 mean in rap?

The Roland TR-808 Rhythm Composer, released in 1980 at $1,195 and discontinued in 1983 after about 12,000 units. Roland's engineers generated its sounds with analog synthesis rather than samples, largely because memory chips were expensive, and the result did not convince anyone it was a real drum kit. That was the commercial problem. It became the artistic point.

The bass drum is the reason the number survives. It was built from a sine oscillator, a low-pass filter and an amplifier, with a decay control that let you stretch the note. Turn it up and the kick stops being a thump and becomes a sustained low tone you feel in your chest.

Marvin Gaye's "Sexual Healing" in 1982 was the first US hit built on the machine. Afrika Bambaataa and the Soulsonic Force's "Planet Rock," also 1982, made it a hip-hop instrument. Producer Hank Shocklee gave the shortest possible summary of what happened next: "it's not hip-hop without that sound."

So when a rapper says the 808s are knocking, they are talking about the bass. Not the box. Almost nobody in a modern session has touched actual 1980s hardware; they are using a sample or a plugin of it, tuned to a musical key and played like a bass guitar. The 808 stopped being a drum machine and became a noun for low end.

## What does beef mean in rap?

A sustained rivalry between artists, argued in records. The word is old and not remotely hip-hop specific. Merriam-Webster dates the "complaint" sense of beef to 1860.

What rap added is the format. A beef generates diss tracks, a term Wikipedia describes as an abbreviation of disrespect, and the exchange becomes the entertainment. The Roxanne Wars, kicked off in 1984 when Roxanne Shanté and Marley Marl answered U.T.F.O., is the usual first citation and spiraled into dozens of response records. The Bridge Wars followed, with MC Shan's "The Bridge" in 1985 and Boogie Down Productions answering with "South Bronx" in 1986 and "The Bridge Is Over" in 1987.

The rules of engagement have changed. The structure has not. Somebody makes a claim in 16 bars, somebody answers in 16 bars, and the audience scores it.

## Rap glossary: 10 more terms worth knowing

**Flow.** The rhythm and rhyme of a delivery, and how those interact with the beat. The single most-used craft word in rap criticism and the hardest to define precisely.

**Hook.** The chorus. Wikipedia defines a hook generally as "a musical or lyrical phrase that stands out and is easily remembered"; in rap it sits between verses and usually runs eight bars.

**Ad-lib.** The short vocal punctuation layered around the main vocal, often panned or doubled. See our [full guide to rap ad-libs](/rap-ad-libs-explained).

**Producer tag.** The audio signature dropped at the top of a beat so you know who made it. The producer's version of a logo, explained in our [rundown of famous producer tags](/producer-tags-explained).

**Freestyle.** Contested by design. Big Daddy Kane described the 1980s meaning as a written rhyme "free of style," meaning no fixed subject. By the early 1990s it had shifted to mean improvised, a change Myka 9 attributed to Freestyle Fellowship treating rap "like a jazz solo."

**Cypher.** An informal circle of rappers trading verses. Reputations get made in them.

**Punchline.** A line built to land on a specific hit, usually the last few beats of a bar. The setup is the first half; the payoff is the rhyme word.

**Feature.** A guest appearance. Usually one verse. Usually 16 bars. See above.

**Double-time.** Rapping at twice the rhythmic density without changing the tempo. Same bars, twice the syllables.

**Slime and slatt.** Two of the most-searched terms in modern rap slang, both traceable to a specific scene and a specific decade. We break down [what "slime" means in rap](/what-does-slime-mean-in-rap) and [what "slatt" means](/what-does-slatt-mean) separately, because both have origin stories worth getting right.
